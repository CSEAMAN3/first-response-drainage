Heavy rainfall puts drainage systems under considerably more pressure than normal day-to-day use.

When a large amount of water enters the drainage network within a short period of time, problems that have previously gone unnoticed can suddenly become much more obvious. Outside drains may begin to overflow, water can collect around gullies and inspection chambers can fill more quickly than usual.

Sometimes this is simply the result of an unusually high volume of rainfall. In other cases, heavy rain can expose a partial blockage, damaged pipe or another underlying drainage problem.

So, how can you tell the difference?

In this guide, we'll look at how heavy rain affects your drains, the warning signs worth paying attention to and when it may be time to have the drainage system professionally investigated.

---

## Why Can Heavy Rain Cause Drainage Problems?

Drainage systems are designed to carry water safely away from our homes and businesses.

During prolonged or particularly intense rainfall, however, considerably more water may enter drains and sewers within a short period of time.

Surface water runs from roofs, driveways, roads and other hard surfaces, while the surrounding ground can eventually become saturated and absorb less rainfall.

The drainage network therefore has much more water to deal with than during normal conditions.

A healthy drainage system should generally cope with everyday rainfall, but periods of unusually heavy rain can expose restrictions, damage and other problems that weren't obvious during drier weather.

---

## Why Does My Outside Drain Overflow When It Rains?

An overflowing outside drain doesn't automatically mean there's a serious problem with the pipe itself.

The first thing to consider is whether the drain only struggles during exceptionally heavy rainfall or whether the problem also occurs during normal conditions.

If water drains away again once the rainfall eases, the drainage system may simply have been dealing with a temporarily high volume of water.

If the water remains, repeatedly backs up or the drain also struggles during dry weather, there could be an obstruction restricting the flow.

Leaves, silt, grease and other debris can reduce the capacity of a drain long before it becomes completely blocked.

When heavy rain arrives, that reduced capacity suddenly becomes much more noticeable.

## Heavy Rain Can Reveal a Partial Blockage

A drain doesn't need to be completely blocked to cause problems.

A pipe may continue carrying enough wastewater during normal day-to-day use even when its capacity has gradually been reduced by a build-up of debris.

Heavy rainfall changes the situation.

As more water enters the system, the restricted pipe may no longer be able to carry it away quickly enough.

Water can then begin to:

- Pool around outside drains
- Rise inside inspection chambers
- Drain away unusually slowly
- Back up through gullies
- Overflow onto surrounding areas

The rain hasn't necessarily caused the blockage.

Instead, it may have exposed a problem that was already developing.

## Leaves, Mud and Debris Can Make the Problem Worse

Autumn creates another challenge for outside drainage.

Falling leaves, twigs, soil and other garden debris can collect around gullies and drain covers.

During heavy rainfall, this material can be washed towards the drainage system.

A layer of leaves covering an outside gully can restrict water before it even enters the drain, while smaller debris may be carried further into the pipework.

Keeping outside drains and gullies clear is therefore particularly important as we move through autumn.

## What Happens After a Dry Summer?

Heavy rainfall can be particularly revealing when it follows an extended period of hot, dry weather.

During drought conditions, some soils can shrink and move. Older clay drainage pipes may be more susceptible to cracking, displaced joints or other movement as the surrounding ground changes.

We explored this in more detail in our guide to [how summer drought can affect older drainage systems](/blog/how-hot-weather-can-affect-older-drainage-systems).

When significant rainfall eventually returns, the increased flow through the drainage system may expose weaknesses that weren't previously noticeable.

This can make it appear as though the heavy rain has suddenly damaged the drain when, in reality, the underlying problem may have been developing for some time.

## Can Heavy Rain Cause Drains to Back Up?

Yes, although the reason isn't always within the private drainage system serving your property.

During intense rainfall, the wider sewer network may also experience unusually high volumes of water.

If the network cannot carry that water away quickly enough, water levels can rise temporarily.

That's why it's useful to consider what's happening around you.

If only one drain at your property is affected, a local blockage or restriction may be more likely.

If several drains are affected or neighbouring properties are experiencing similar problems at the same time, the issue could be further along the drainage network.

## Warning Signs to Look Out for During Heavy Rain

Seeing water around a drain during an exceptionally heavy downpour isn't necessarily cause for alarm.

What's more important is how the drainage system behaves during and after the rainfall.

Look out for:

- Water that remains around a drain long after the rain stops
- Outside drains repeatedly overflowing
- Water rising unusually high inside inspection chambers
- Gurgling from toilets, sinks or other drains
- Several fixtures draining slowly at the same time
- Unpleasant drainage smells
- The same problem returning every time it rains
- Wastewater backing up towards the property

Repeated symptoms are particularly worth investigating.

## What Should You Do If an Outside Drain Is Overflowing?

If you notice an outside drain overflowing during heavy rainfall, avoid immediately removing inspection covers or attempting to reach into the drainage system.

Instead, start with the visible area around the drain.

If leaves or other surface debris are covering the gully, and it is safe to do so, clearing them away may allow water to enter the drain normally again.

You can then observe what happens as the rainfall eases.

If the water quickly disappears and the problem doesn't return, the drain may simply have been temporarily overwhelmed.

If the water remains or the drain repeatedly overflows, there's a greater chance that something is restricting the system.

## FAQs

### Why does my outside drain overflow when it rains?

An outside drain may overflow because heavy rainfall is temporarily introducing more water than the system can carry away. However, recurring overflowing can also indicate a partial blockage or another restriction within the drainage system.

---

### Does an overflowing drain always mean it's blocked?

No. During exceptionally heavy rainfall, a drainage system may temporarily struggle with the volume of water entering it. If the water doesn't clear after the rain stops or the problem happens regularly, it's worth investigating for a blockage or underlying defect.

---

### Can leaves cause an outside drain to overflow?

Yes. Leaves and other garden debris can cover gullies and prevent surface water from entering the drainage system efficiently. Smaller debris can also enter the drain and contribute to an obstruction.

---

### Why do my drains only smell when it rains?

Changes in water levels and airflow within the drainage system can sometimes make existing drainage odours more noticeable during wet weather. Persistent smells, particularly alongside slow drainage or overflowing, can indicate a problem that needs investigating.

---

### Can heavy rain damage underground drains?

Heavy rainfall doesn't necessarily damage a healthy drainage system. However, increased water flow can expose existing weaknesses such as cracked pipes, displaced joints or partial collapses.

---

### Should I call a drainage company if my drain only overflows during heavy rain?

If it happens once during exceptionally severe rainfall and the water quickly clears afterwards, professional intervention may not be necessary.

If it happens repeatedly, remains after the rain stops or is accompanied by other drainage symptoms, having the system investigated is sensible.
